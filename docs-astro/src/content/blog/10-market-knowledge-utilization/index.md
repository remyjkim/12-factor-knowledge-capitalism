A market is not merely a place to trade. It is a machine for *utilizing* knowledge. Its deepest function, as [Hayek](https://home.uchicago.edu/~vlima/courses/econ200/spring01/hayek.pdf) saw, is to marshal knowledge no single mind holds. Each price aggregates the dispersed, fragmentary understanding of everyone who acts on it and routes resources toward their most valued use, with no one commanding the whole. The knowledge market itself is peculiar, a [long-tail](https://www.wired.com/2004/10/tail/), [high-verification-cost](https://www.sfu.ca/~allen/Ackerlof.pdf) marketplace where most value lies in a vast tail of specialized claims, each costly to confirm. Its liquidity is augmented by *routing and matchmaking*. [Search engines](http://infolab.stanford.edu/pub/papers/google.pdf) and ad markets are, at bottom, machines for matching a query to the knowledge that answers it. And because verification is costly, transacting knowledge demands primitives ordinary goods do not. One is [Bayesian persuasion](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/BayesianPersuasion.pdf), how an informed party credibly shapes another's beliefs by committing in advance to a disclosure rule. Another is [garbling](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-mathematical-statistics/volume-24/issue-2/Equivalent-Comparisons-of-Experiments/10.1214/aoms/1177729032.full), deliberately coarsening information to reveal just enough to transact without giving the whole away. These are how a market sells what it cannot fully show, turning latent knowledge into utilized knowledge.
